# NMPAN Mobile Slaughter Unit Manual (May 2010): lessons relevant to failure modes

Source: NMPAN, *Mobile Slaughter Unit Manual*, May 2010 (PDF hosted by nichemeatprocessing.org). Mostly a compliance guide (model HACCP, SSOPs, regulatory walk-throughs) based on operating USDA-inspected MSUs. This extraction keeps only the parts that bear on why MSUs stall or underperform. Contacts listed: Lauren Gwin (Oregon State) and Arion Thiboumery (Iowa State). The data is from 2010 and may be out of date.

## The fixed-facility dependency

- "Every MSU must partner with a fixed facility ('cut and wrap') where further processing is done." The MSU operator either runs that facility too, and so carries all of its compliance, or contracts with a separately owned plant that handles its own compliance.
- Taos County Economic Development Corporation (TCEDC, NM) "Mobile Matanza": unit completed in 2006 and granted federal inspection in May 2008. Its cut-and-wrap was TCEDC's 5,000 sf Taos Food Center commercial kitchen, which "came under inspection soon after." NMED rejected TCEDC's planned on-site anaerobic wastewater treatment and required a town sewer hookup ($3,000). That is two inspected facilities to keep running, not one.
- Coast Grown (CA) had no cut-and-wrap of its own. It partnered with a separately owned USDA-inspected processor, and "ranchers arrange cut and wrap and pickup of products directly with the processor."

## Idle-unit case: Coast Grown parked seven years

- "This MSU ... was built in 2002 by ranchers in the Central Coast region of California ... Regulatory complexities paired with uncertain markets kept it parked for seven years, but in 2009, it finally began operating as a USDA-inspected mobile slaughter unit."
- At its first humane slaughter audit (summer 2009) "It was their 9th operating day, and they had harvested 32 animals total since beginning operations under inspection." That is about 3.6 head per operating day [inferred arithmetic].
- Water: 300 gallons per 2-3 beef. Using more than 300 gallons per ranch site triggered another NRCS review of the wash-water management plan.

## Offal and blood disposal varies by state

- New Mexico: TCEDC "worked with NMED and FSIS to establish that offal is recognized as the responsibility of the rancher, and that the rancher may compost it on his own land." Bones may go to landfill.
- California: "does not permit on-ranch composting of offal (or any mammalian tissue; see CA ... Section 17855.2)." CCAC needs a CDFA "inedibles" permit naming an approved rendering plant, pet food plant, or collection station, and must haul offal there.
- Island Grown (WA): "Inedible offal and blood from livestock are composted at the respective farms."
- Model SSOP: "Blood clots, rumen contents, and any manure will be removed and handled by the ranch." Waste is pushed onto the host farm.
- [inferred] Virginia's rules on offal composting would decide whether a Shenandoah Valley MSU needs a renderer contract. Unverified here.

## Inspection variability (FSA case)

- Island Grown Farmers Cooperative MSU (WA, operating since 2002) had Food Safety Assessments in 2008 and 2009. The 2009 FSA ran "mid-January 2009 ... until mid-April." The EIAO required more E. coli O157:H7 testing than IGFC's lab could do and pushed in-house testing. Even with donated equipment, "in-house testing would still cost more" than an outside lab. IGFC eventually found a different lab.
- Lessons: "EIAOs are not all alike and may interpret the same regulation differently"; "It just takes time." Disputes should go to FSIS OPPD (Omaha), not the district office.
- FSA frequency at that time was at least once every 4 years per establishment.

## Other overhead that lands on a small operator

- Driving the tractor-trailer needs a Class A CDL plus state DOT drug/alcohol testing and background checks (TCEDC, CCAC).
- California also required registration in the CHP Biennial Inspection of Terminals program, a CDFA meat license on top of USDA inspection, CDFA brand registration, and hide tags with ownership affidavits.
- Potable water: monthly testing (NM) or annual testing of the source plus biannual testing of private wells at each ranch site (CA). The MSU must carry a potability certificate for each location.
- Food defense: TCEDC was subject to state Homeland Security requirements. At IGFC, keys held across "staff, board, and members" had to be collected, which is a governance/control issue in a coop.
- Advice (Deb Garrison, former CCAC coordinator): learn the codes before talking to regulators, because new agency staff "will unknowingly give you wrong information, which can be costly."
