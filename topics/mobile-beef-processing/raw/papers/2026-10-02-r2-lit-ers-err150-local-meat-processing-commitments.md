# Local Meat and Poultry Processing: The Importance of Business Commitments for Long-Term Viability (ERS ERR-150, 2013)

**Evidence type:** government research report (USDA Economic Research Service, Economic Research Report No. 150, June 2013), internally reviewed by ERS and AMS. It combines a cost model with seven processor case studies. Fetched from the AgEcon Search mirror via Wayback, because ers.usda.gov's old webdocs URL now 404s and AgEcon Search returns a bot challenge. Two of the authors (Gwin, Thiboumery) coordinate NMPAN, and Thiboumery was VP of Lorentz Meats, one of the case studies.

- Assumes average processing revenue of "$500 per beef, $150 per hog, $150 per sheep or goat" (2013 dollars).
- Staffing: very small plant 4 FTE, small 10 FTE, regional 60 FTE with benefits.
- "Individual plants at any one of the three scales could likely be viable with 10 percent more or less revenue/livestock." The models "are not meant to be precise."
- "opening a new processing facility cannot be done without commitments of significant livestock volume and significant capital."

## Background box on mobile slaughter units (pp. 7-8)
- Advantages: MSUs go to the farm, "shifting some transportation costs from the farmer to the MSU operator and reducing stress for livestock"; may be cheaper to build than a fixed slaughter floor; "appear to be easier to site, given zoning restrictions and community resistance."
- Disadvantages: "they may cost more to operate on a per-head basis because of fuel and maintenance costs, time spent in transit, and limited capacity over which to spread those costs." Carcasses must go to an inspected cut-and-wrap facility, and "some MSU operators have found that the total cost of processing in two separate facilities exceeds the cost of doing it all at one."
- Basic red meat MSU: two rooms (processing room and cooler), pulled by a semi-tractor or pickup. Multi-unit systems need level docking stations with utilities, water, pens, and chutes.
- Inspected MSUs must meet HACCP and normal sanitation rules, "with the notable exception that (in most USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service districts) slaughter and bleed-out may occur outside." Footnote: FSIS MSU Guidance (2010) allows this, but "Districts are not required to follow the Guidance."
- First inspected red-meat MSU: Broken Arrow Ranch, Texas, 1983, under Texas state inspection. At least 10 inspected MSUs were operating at the time of writing.
- Success factors: "(a) a committed relationship (ideally joint operation if not ownership) with an inspected cut-and-wrap facility; (b) one or two leaders who are highly dedicated ... and (c) appropriate scale." Growth "limits their mobility and agility."
- Footnote 2, custom-exempt trucks: "mobile slaughter trucks that operate under USDA's custom exemption and have been around for decades: the butcher on these trucks goes to the farm, slaughters the livestock, and delivers cleaned carcasses to a custom-exempt cut-and-wrap facility." The meat is for the owner, family, employees, and nonpaying guests only; farmers can pre-sell animals "on the hoof".
- "outcomes for MSUs depend on commitments from farmers to keep them busy."

## PSMPC / Heritage Meats (footnote 14)
- The PSMPC MSU began inspected operation in 2009, with Heritage Meats doing cut-and-wrap. Use came in "significantly lower than originally estimated" because (a) direct marketing is hard and high commodity prices led farmers not to direct market, and (b) other inspected plants began serving small producers.

## Seasonality, scheduling, cost
- Farmers who call "a month or two before" often find processors fully booked in peak season; fall game processing (charged at a premium) makes it worse. Processors face a "boom-and-bust" cycle; "Seasonality of supply is a significant drain on processor profitability."
- No-shows: "lost productivity and lost revenue for the processor, with little or no recourse."
- Regulatory compliance and offal disposal cost small plants disproportionately; for many, "the drop" (offal) "is a liability rather than a revenue source."
- Table 5 (20 grass-fed cattle, 660-lb carcasses, 62% yield): processing at $0.65/lb is 13% of an $8.00/lb local retail price; marketing, distribution, and retail margins are 44%. Livestock trucking 100 miles at $3.50/loaded mile = $350.
- Ranch Foods Direct case: started with a $2 million plant, $500,000 equipment, $100,000 operating capital, and lost more than $1 million in 3 years.

## Relevance to a Shenandoah Valley mobile beef unit [inferred]
- The very-small model ($231,000/yr, about 462 beef/yr at $500) is the nearest published benchmark for a mobile unit plus cut-and-wrap partner. Adjusted for 2013-to-2026 inflation, both the cost and the per-head fee would be higher. The ratio (about 9 head/week) is the useful number.
- A custom-exempt mobile kill truck is the decades-old model the report separates from inspected MSUs, and it fits Virginia's custom-exempt route.
