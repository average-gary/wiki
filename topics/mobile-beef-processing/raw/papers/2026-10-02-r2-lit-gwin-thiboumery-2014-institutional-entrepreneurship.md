# Beyond the farmer and the butcher (Gwin & Thiboumery, 2014)

**Evidence type:** peer-reviewed journal article, qualitative case studies (JAFSCD 4(2), 81-96). The authors founded and coordinate NMPAN, so they are not neutral observers. Earlier versions of the case studies came from a USDA ERS cooperative agreement. Evidence is from interviews and is described by the authors as "tentative".

## Framing
- Processing "is often said to be the weak link" for local meat. Facilities "may be located far from farms and ranches, have limited availability during peak livestock finishing periods (especially the fall for ruminant livestock), or lack the inspection status, skills, or services desired by producers."
- "meat processing is a high-risk, thin-margin business."
- Custom-exempt is defined (footnote 1) as not continuously inspected; "the meat cannot be sold and is for the use of the owner of the animal," citing Johnson, Marti, & Gwin (2012), which is ERS LDP-M-216-01.

## Vermont / New England
- New England working group coordinator Chelsea Bardot Lewis interviewed 20 of the region's 28 inspected processors: "After the first three, it was clear that their biggest problem wasn't regulation... It was not having enough supply, enough animals over the course of the year." Workforce was the other core problem; both made worse by seasonality (Lewis & Peters, 2011).
- A Northeast Organic Farming Association survey of Vermont processors: seasonality was critical, and "storage and cut-and-wrap capacity appeared to be much tighter bottlenecks than slaughter capacity."
- Conclusion drawn: fixing inefficiencies in existing infrastructure "was likely to be more effective than trying to add plants in places that appeared to be 'processing deserts'." Lewis: "Everyone wants some graphic that shows ... here's a gap, so let's put a plant here. That's not the right approach."
- 2010 Vermont financial assessment of small processors: thin operating margins, labor and energy the main costs, undercapitalized, substantial debt, no productivity or financial tracking.
- 2011: Vermont legislature provided US$50,000 in matching funds. One grant paid for a rail system renovation that raised capacity 40 percent; another paid for a hot-water tank and pasteurizer. Mad River Food Hub added federally inspected cutting rooms to ease the cut-and-wrap bottleneck.
- Outcomes (as of Aug. 2013, per Vermont Meat Inspection Service): state-inspected plants grew from 3 (2005) to 16; three custom-exempt plants upgraded to state inspection in the prior year; producers committing to slaughter dates into the coming year.

## North Carolina (NC Choices)
- Of the state's 20 small-scale processors (custom-exempt, USDA, and state), only a few offered value-added processing and the cutting, packaging and labeling producers needed.
- Acre Station Meat Farm went from 10 to more than 25 employees and processes for 80 producers; it is up to 3 hours' drive for many producers.
- Weaver Street Market whole-animal pilot (2008-2010) raised weekly local grass-fed beef and pastured pork sales 150 percent, but most buyers lack the equipment and skill to buy whole carcasses.

## New York (NELPSC)
- Founded 2005 with a US$52,000 NY Agriculture and Markets grant; LLC with a producer board and one paid employee.
- "Processing facilitation" for a one-time US$50 fee: matches producers to processors, schedules slaughter dates, passes on cutting instructions. In 2012: more than 130 producer clients, agreements with 11 processors (USDA-inspected and state-licensed custom-exempt).
- Grant-subsidized until 2010; became self-sustaining from its wholesale marketing arm (Local Foods from Local Farms), which pays by hanging weight.

## National (NMPAN)
- Resources include business planning tools, regulatory guides, and "mobile slaughter unit videos and guidebook". Start-up funding was US$300,000 over five years (Kellogg, Heifer, USDA RD, eXtension).

## Scheduling
- "producers may have to book slaughter spots long in advance, yet processors often have costly no-shows that leave employees and equipment idle" (see Gwin et al., 2013).
- Efforts to change federal inspection law to favor small plants "have gained little traction"; state and county administrative fixes have worked better.

## Relevance to a Shenandoah Valley mobile beef unit [inferred]
- A mobile slaughter unit adds kill capacity only. If cut-and-wrap and cold storage are the real bottleneck, as the Vermont evidence suggests, the unit needs a contracted or owned cut-and-wrap and hanging-cooler partner, or it just moves the queue downstream.
- Fall seasonality and no-shows are the main revenue risks. Deposits or committed dates (the NELPSC-style coordination) are the documented fix.
