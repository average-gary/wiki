# Island Grown Farmers Cooperative (IGFC) - NMPAN case study

Page header says "Updated 3.29.2018", but most numbers inside are 2007-2011 vintage (fees "spring '08", membership "2011"). Treat them as historical, not current. [inferred: the page was refreshed in 2018 without its figures being re-dated.]

## Basics
- First USDA-inspected mobile slaughter facility for red meat in the US. It received its grant of inspection and began operating in **2002**. Further processing is done at a permanent USDA-inspected plant in **Bow, WA**.
- **Capacity:** "about 9-10 head beef (or 35 lamb or 15 pigs)" per day. That takes 2 butchers 8 hours, plus 2 hours of driving.
- The unit runs only **4 days/week**, because of limited staff and because each day's meat has to go back to the plant, followed by truck and trailer cleaning and maintenance. It runs 52 weeks/year at 3-4 days/week.
- Up to 8 hours/day under inspection, plus set-up and clean-up time.
- The plant runs 5 days/week and processes **2,500 lb/day**. The trailer is 34 ft long; the plant is 3,000 sf.
- Services: slaughter and processing, raw sausage, case-ready and retail packaging. Certified organic (Washington Dept of Agriculture). The co-op does custom work "rarely, because too busy with inspected work."
- Staff: 6 employees. Annual sales revenue: **$500,000** (all services, not counting the value of the meat).

## Fees (spring 2008 basis)
- Slaughter: $40 lamb/goat, $55 pig, **$105 steer**.
- A farm needs at least **$450** of slaughter work for the unit to visit.
- Cutting to case-ready: $1.05/lb lamb, **$0.82/lb steer**, $0.6071/lb pig, "plus 10% price increase, spring '08". Link sausage: $1.25/lb. Non-members pay slightly higher rates.
- Operating cost: about **$294,500/yr**. "Fee structure is designed to break even or be slightly profitable." The truck gets about 10 mpg.
- On-site retail of members' meat, open 2 days/week: about $9,000/month.

## Ownership and history
- Started in 1996 as talks among San Juan County farmers and county extension. The farmers had no practical way to get animals to mainland USDA plants.
- The **Lopez Community Land Trust** (LCLT) hosted the project and hired Bruce Dunlop to design and build the unit. IGFC was formed specifically to operate it and later bought it from LCLT.
- IGFC is a **service co-op with no brand of its own**. Members market their own meat. There were 30 founding members, each paying a $600 capital charge. Membership was 65 in 2011, and about 95% of members use the co-op at least once a year.
- Member farms all sit within **100 miles (1-2 hours' drive)**, "the largest area the MPU can serve efficiently."
- A volunteer board meets monthly. The head butcher manages both the unit and the plant.

## Capital
- Total project cost was **$150,000 in 2000**: trailer $60k, equipment and installation $27k, truck $18k, design/project management $25k, testing $15k, outreach $5k.
- A new trailer of the same capacity in 2008: **$170,000**.
- Funding came from USDA grants (CSREES, Rural Development, Rural Business Opportunity), a $20k Forest Service grant that paid for the truck and refrigeration, and $80-90k from private donors. There was no initial debt.
- IGFC rents the cut-and-wrap building. It bought the equipment from the landlord, funded by an equity retain charged on each slaughter, and paid it off in 4 years.
- For a later expansion, members lent the co-op money themselves at a slightly lower rate than a bank loan.

## Lessons
- The original rates, based on an industry standard, "were too low: after six months, they were losing money". Rates have been raised several times since.
- The co-op broke even in year 1: "We had to break even because we didn't have money to lose."
- "Central to their success ... none of these farmers has any other options for slaughter/processing."
- Regulatory work: two members took the required HACCP class and wrote the plan from the USDA generic model. The HACCP plan and the SSOPs (sanitation procedures) are separate documents. "Every inspector has his own interpretation". The HACCP coordinator is a critical job.
- The unit needed no county permits, "because it isn't a building". Rinse water (about 300 gal per farm visit) and offal are composted on-farm.
- The unit is self-contained: diesel generator, water storage, hot water, refrigeration. Carcasses reach temperature by the next morning.
- **Labor is 75% of total costs.** Wages ran $11-22/hr, with skilled cutters at $18-19/hr. Health insurance was added in 2008 to keep staff. A good cutter takes about 2 years on the job to train.
- Business is slow February through April, and employees are encouraged to take unpaid time off then.
- The biggest labor challenge was management. The head butcher had no management experience, and board members took over scheduling, which is "critical to cash flow."
- Throughput: more than **300,000 lb of meat in 2007**, with a retail value of $1,044,000. Most members sell fewer than 50 beef/year; a few sell 100-200.
- Expanding the cut-and-wrap plant nearly doubled its hanging-room capacity, but it was maxed out again. The co-op was limiting new members because of space and staff.
- Key structural point for a business plan: the **mobile unit only kills and chills**. The bottleneck is the fixed hanging and cutting facility.
