# NMPAN: Wastewater Treatment for Meat Processors

The page was last updated September 9, 2015. The attached data tables are Kennedy/Jenks Consultants, November 2013, drawing in part on NMPAN member processors. Data PDF: https://www.nichemeatprocessing.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Kennedy_Jenks-Tables-Nov.2013.pdf

## Planning questions NMPAN says to answer before calling an engineer

- Activities: slaughter only, cut and wrap, or value-added.
- Species processed.
- Whether a municipal connection is possible.
- Daily volume, broken down by activity.
- Wastewater characteristics: pH, TSS, BOD, and FOG.

Example given: a small Indiana poultry and red-meat plant uses "about 1,600 gallons/day, with a wastewater BOD level of 1,000-1,200 mg/L going into their treatment system."

## Options as NMPAN describes them

- **Municipal sewer.** "In general, if it is possible to connect to municipal wastewater treatment, this is often your best bet ... in the long run it will probably be worth it to pay the initial connection fees and monthly sewer costs rather than build and manage your own." Before contacting the utility, know your volume, pH, TSS, BOD, and FOG.
- **Septic.** "A typical septic system will probably not work for most meat processing plants because of the high levels of BOD, TSS, and FOG in the wastewater."
- **Constructed wetland.** Brushy Prairie Packing at Gunthorp Farms, Indiana, is a USDA-inspected poultry and red-meat plant. Greg Gunthorp: "Our system consists of six 1,000-gallon tanks for separation prior to the wetland. The first tank draws off the bottom as a grease trap. The remainder are set up as normal septic tanks pulling out of the middle. There is an effluent filter at the end of the septic tanks. The rest rooms go into a separate 1,000-gallon septic tank and enter the system just ahead of the constructed wetland." He adds: "Blood, fat, hair, and feathers are rough on waste treatment systems ... If a facility is not going to catch the blood, fat, hair, or feathers or have some means to separate them out, the waste water stream is going to be significantly stronger and take a much, much larger system." The system was permitted with the LaGrange County Health Department (NMPAN webinar, July 23, 2013).
- **Anaerobic digesters.** NMPAN has not "found an anaerobic digester system that is cost-effective for a small processing facility". It notes meat waste "isn't a very good substrate".
- **Ponds and lagoons, aerated or anaerobic.** Wastewater must be pretreated first. Lagoons emit odors and are "usually only suitable for rural, isolated areas." NMPAN advises separating streams: kill-floor water "will have blood and other materials leading to high BOD levels," while processing-room water carries cleaning chemicals. If effluent goes to a field, plan for the rainy season.

## Pretreatment ("A Note on Pre-treatment")

"All of the systems above, even connecting to a municipal system, require some form of pre-treatment."

Pretreatment ranges from management practices ("sweeping up the kill floor and processing floor before wash down to capture any solids, capturing blood on the kill floor so it doesn't go down the drain"), to simple filters, to advanced systems. "The main goal of pre-treatment is to limit what you 'send down the line.' Blood, fat and tissue are all nutrient rich materials ... Removal = cost."

## Relevance to the two Virginia sites [inferred]

- **(a) On-farm kill by a mobile unit.** Capturing blood is the single biggest lever on BOD. Compare WA plant 3974 at 127 mg/l, with blood hauled off, against 1,000-2,500 mg/l where blood goes down the drain. Captured blood can travel with offal to rendering or composting, both covered in earlier rounds.
- **(b) Cooler site where the mobile unit docks.** Wash-down water there is mostly processing-room and equipment water, so its strength should be lower than kill-floor water. That is still not demonstrated. A first tank drawn off the bottom as a grease and solids interceptor, plus an effluent filter and a separate domestic tank, is the small-plant pattern NMPAN documents.
- **Planning load.** At 50-200 gal per beef, 5 head per day means 250-1,000 gpd. That straddles the 1,000 gpd line between small and large AOSS in 12VAC5-613.
